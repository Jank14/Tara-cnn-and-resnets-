Move W first, then b — notice W changes the SPREAD (variance) of the output, while b only slides the CENTER. You need both to match a target that's both wider and shifted.
Why this always works for Gaussian → Gaussian: W·X+b is just SCALE (by W) then SHIFT (by b) — an affine map. A Gaussian, scaled and shifted, is provably still exactly a Gaussian. That's the whole reason linear is enough here: it can reach any mean and any variance, and that's all a Gaussian's shape has.

The target this time is TWO-HUMPED (bimodal) — something a single linear transform can NEVER produce, no matter what W and b you pick, because scaling+shifting a bell curve always leaves it a single bell curve. Flip the activation toggle on and watch what becomes possible.
The link back to Tab 1: linear (W·X+b) can only ever SCALE and SHIFT — that's it, two knobs, mean and variance. It can never change the SHAPE of the distribution. A single bell curve, however scaled or shifted, is still one bell curve. Getting a genuinely different shape (two humps) needs something non-linear — that's exactly what the activation toggle adds.
